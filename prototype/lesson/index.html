Logistic regression

Predict the probability of a yes-or-no outcome by passing a through the .

In plain words

Think about whether to take an umbrella. You look at clues (clouds, humidity, the forecast), give each one some weight, and end up with a feeling somewhere between “surely not” and “surely yes”. Logistic regression does the same with numbers: it adds up the clues and turns the total into a chance between 0% and 100%.

Step by step

1Why not just a straight line?

With you could fit a line to yes-or-no data and round at 0.5. It works until one extra far-away example drags the line and moves the whole . It can also output values below 0 or above 1, which make no sense as a chance.

In the lecture

The instructor adds one obvious example to the data and shows the fitted line, and the threshold with it, shifting even though the pattern is still clear.

2Squash the score into a probability

Keep the linear score, then pass it through the (also called the logistic function). Whatever the score, the output lands between 0 and 1.

z = θ·x
p = g(z) = 1 / (1 + e^−z)
3Read the output as a chance

The output is the probability that the label is 1. The two chances add up to 1: if a tumor has a 10% chance of being malignant, it has a 90% chance of being benign. That is how turns a score into a decision.

P(y = 1 | x) = p
P(y = 0 | x) = 1 − p
4Pick the weights that make the data most likely

Choose θ so the training labels are as probable as possible. For convenience we take the log, which gives the .

ℓ(θ) = Σ [ y·log(p) + (1 − y)·log(1 − p) ]
5Climb to the best weights

Now we want the highest point, not the lowest, so we use : the same idea as gradient descent with a plus sign instead of a minus. For logistic regression the log-likelihood has a single peak, so there are no local optima to get stuck on.

θ ← θ + α · (y − p) · x
